# Point of View Writing Style Guide

Purpose:

Help agents write sharp, human, evidence-led points of view without drifting into generic AI prose.

## What A Good POV Sounds Like

A strong POV is a clear argument about how the world is changing.

It should have:

- a specific claim that could be debated
- a reason the claim matters now
- a mechanism that explains why the change is happening
- evidence, signals, or examples
- a clear view of who wins, who loses, and what changes next
- a counterargument that is taken seriously
- practical implications for leaders

## Core Writing Rules

Use concrete language.

Prefer:

- "AI removes the junior analyst bottleneck in consulting delivery."
- "Clients will pay for judgment, trust, and implementation accountability, not for slide production."

Avoid:

- "AI is transforming the consulting landscape."
- "Organizations must embrace innovation to unlock value."

Make every paragraph pass this test:

- What is the claim?
- Why should I believe it?
- Why does it matter?

## Ban Generic AI Writing Patterns

Avoid these patterns unless there is a specific reason:

- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape..."
- "It is not just X, it is Y."
- "This underscores the importance of..."
- "Organizations must navigate..."
- "Unlock value."
- "Leverage synergies."
- "Robust and scalable."
- "Seamless integration."
- "Game-changing."
- "Delve into."
- "At the heart of..."
- "A testament to..."
- "Poised to revolutionize."
- "Critical enabler."
- "Holistic approach."

Avoid automatic three-part lists where every sentence has the same rhythm.
Avoid ending every section with a generic "businesses must adapt" conclusion.

## Sentence Style

Use varied sentence length.

Prefer short direct claims, then explain:

- "The pyramid is cracking. AI can now perform much of the leverage work that made consulting economics attractive."

Use longer sentences only when they carry real reasoning.

Avoid heavy punctuation as decoration. Use commas and periods before stylistic dashes.

## Evidence Discipline

Separate:

- observed facts
- sourced evidence
- inferred hypotheses
- speculative bets

Do not pretend a hypothesis is proven.
Use confidence levels when evidence is incomplete.

Good phrasing:

- "The evidence suggests..."
- "A defensible hypothesis is..."
- "This should be validated by..."

Weak phrasing:

- "It is clear that..."
- "Without question..."
- "Everyone knows..."

## Plain-English Test

After writing each POV, explain it as if to a smart 12-year-old.

The explanation should answer:

- What is changing?
- Why is it changing?
- Why should someone care?
- What would happen if the POV is right?

If the 12-year-old version is clearer than the executive version, rewrite the executive version.

## POV Structure

For each POV, use this structure:

1. Name: short, memorable, not cute.
2. Sharp claim: one sentence.
3. Why now: the trigger or timing.
4. Mechanism: how the change works.
5. Evidence: facts, examples, signals, or research needs.
6. Winners and losers: who benefits and who is exposed.
7. Counterargument: the strongest objection.
8. Implications: what leaders should do.
9. Confidence: high, medium, or low, with reason.

## Consultant-Specific Guidance

For consulting, professional services, or advisory topics:

- distinguish work production from judgment
- distinguish deliverables from outcomes
- analyze the leverage model, partner economics, staffing model, client trust, and implementation accountability
- avoid assuming AI kills all consulting
- ask which parts of consulting become cheaper, which become more valuable, and which become indefensible
